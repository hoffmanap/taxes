Does Density Pay For Itself?
Population Density, Land Area & the Cost of City Government

A statistical look at whether denser cities deliver municipal services more cheaply per resident, whether that shows up in the property tax bill, and whether the answer differs service by service. Fifty largest U.S. cities, ranked by 2022 population.

Cities analyzed: 50 Service categories: 16 Finance data: U.S. Census Bureau, 2022 Census of Governments — Individual Unit Finance File Geography: 2023 Census Gazetteer (land area)

01Headline Result

The relationship runs the opposite direction from the density-efficiency hypothesis — but only in aggregate. Across the 50 largest cities, higher density is associated with higher per-capita municipal spending and higher per-capita property tax collection, not lower. But pooling every service together hides real differentiation: some services show no density penalty at all (§04), the tax relationship turns out to be almost entirely explained by the spending relationship rather than an independent effect of density itself (§05), and a city's housing mix — not density itself — turns out to be the sharper explanation for spending differences (§03). Separately, home value, not density, is what drives the effective property tax rate homeowners actually feel — and that's where El Paso stands out (§06).

03A Sharper Lens Than Density: Housing Type

The question here: "density" (people per square mile) treats a city of small single-family lots and a city of apartment towers the same, as long as they pack in the same number of people. But those are very different things to build and service. So we also pulled the share of each city's housing that's single-family detached homes — the more direct stand-in for "spread-out, low-rise, one-house-per-lot" versus "compact, multifamily, built-up" — and tested it the same way.

It works even better than density does — and once it's in the picture, density stops mattering on its own. The share of a city's homes that are single-family explains municipal spending per person more precisely than density does (60% of the city-to-city variation in total spending vs. 41% for density; 47% vs. 37% for the density-sensitive service bundle from §04). And when both are put in the same model together, density's own effect drops to essentially nothing — statistically indistinguishable from zero — while housing type stays strong and significant. In plain terms: it was never really "density" driving the cost pattern. It was housing type. Density looked like it mattered because dense cities also tend to be apartment-heavy cities — but housing type is the more fundamental variable, and density was riding along with it.
The direction is the same as before, just sharper: cities with a higher share of single-family homes spend less per person, not more. Cities built more around apartments and multifamily housing spend more. That's consistent with everything in §02–§04 — it doesn't reverse the earlier finding, it explains it better. This is also the opposite of the common "sprawl costs more" intuition — in this data, the single-family, lower-density cities are the cheaper ones to run per resident, at least at the level of the whole city budget.

One place this does not help: the effective tax rate story in §06. Home value alone already explains about 40% of the city-to-city difference in effective tax rate, and adding housing type on top of that explains essentially nothing more (p=0.39). El Paso's high effective tax rate is a home-value story, not a housing-type story — even though El Paso is also heavily single-family, that fact isn't what's pushing its tax rate up. Housing type strengthens the spending side of the analysis (§02–§04); it doesn't add anything new to the tax-rate side (§06).

04By Service Type — Which Services Cost More in Denser Cities?

In plain terms: we tested all 16 categories of city spending separately against density. Not one of them gets cheaper per person as density rises. They split into two groups instead — services that get more expensive per person as a city gets denser, and services where density makes no measurable difference either way. There is no category, in this data, where being denser saves money per resident.

These are the classic "shared pipes and roads" services — exactly where you'd expect density to help. It doesn't cost more per person here, but it doesn't cost less either. Flat, not falling.
Bottom line: the original idea — pack people in tighter and each person's share of the water main or the road gets cheaper — doesn't show up as savings anywhere in this data. At best (sewers, parks, roads, trash, health) density is a wash: no penalty, no discount. Everywhere else — and especially in jails, parking, libraries, housing programs, and general administration — being denser costs more per person, not less. That's why the citywide total in §02 goes up with density: it's pulled up by the administrative and social-service side of the budget, not by physical infrastructure.

05Density, Cost, and Tax — Together

The question here: is the tax-bill pattern in §02 really about density — or is it just riding along with the spending pattern? We already know three things move together: denser cities spend more per person (§02), and cities that spend more also collect more property tax per person — no surprise there, since taxes are largely how spending gets funded. The question is whether density is doing anything on its own to push tax bills up, separate from spending, or whether it's only showing up in the tax numbers because it's already showing up in the spending numbers.

To test that, we hold spending constant and ask: once two cities spend the same amount per person, does the denser one still collect more tax? The answer is no.

In plain terms: density does not directly drive tax bills. It drives spending, and spending drives tax bills. Once you compare cities that spend about the same per person, density stops predicting property tax per person almost entirely — the connection all but disappears. But spending itself still strongly predicts tax bills even after accounting for density. So the real chain is: denser city → spends more per person → collects more property tax per person. Density doesn't have its own separate effect on your tax bill — it only matters because of what it does to spending. If you found two cities that spent the same amount per resident, you would not expect the denser one to have a higher tax bill just because it's denser.

(For the statistically inclined: this was tested with partial correlations. Density vs. spending, controlling for tax, stays strong and significant. Density vs. tax, controlling for spending, drops to essentially zero and stops being statistically significant. Spending vs. tax, controlling for density, stays strong. That pattern — one link survives, the other one vanishes — is what "spending explains it, density doesn't" looks like in the numbers.)

All three variables at once: x = density (log), y = total cost per capita (log), bubble size = property tax per capita. If density and tax moved together independent of cost, you'd expect the biggest bubbles to cluster at the right regardless of height. Instead bubble size tracks the y-axis (cost) far more than the x-axis (density) — visual confirmation of the mediation result above.

06Home Values, Income, and the Real Tax Burden

The question behind this section: a city's property tax bill and its property tax rate are two different things. The bill is dollars collected per person. The rate is the bill divided by what the home is actually worth — and that's what determines whether a homeowner feels squeezed, because two cities can collect the same number of dollars per person while taxing very different fractions of a homeowner's actual property value, depending on how expensive homes are locally.

Home value, not density, is what actually explains the effective tax rate — and it explains it strongly. Cities with cheaper homes charge a much higher tax rate to raise the same money (r = −0.52, statistically strong). Density on its own barely correlates with the rate at all (r = −0.21, not statistically significant) — density only shows up here because denser cities tend to also have pricier homes, and it's the home value doing the work, not the density itself.
El Paso is close to the textbook example of this pattern. Among the 47 cities with complete data, El Paso has below-average density (38th densest of 47) and the 4th-cheapest homes (median value $159,300) — yet the 2nd-highest effective property tax rate in the entire list, at 2.17%. Its actual dollar tax bill per resident is unremarkable — 19th of 47, squarely in the middle of the pack. It's not that El Paso collects an unusual amount of tax money per person; it's that the same, fairly ordinary amount of tax money is being divided by an unusually low home value, which pushes the rate way up. Compare Seattle and San José: both far denser than El Paso, both with home values 5–7× higher, and both collect more tax dollars per person ($990 and $705 vs. El Paso's $506) — while charging a rate roughly a third as high (0.77% and 0.75% vs. 2.17%). Cheap homes, not low density, are why El Paso's rate is high.

09Method & Caveats

Service categories: built from Census of Governments item codes, combining current-operations and capital-construction spending for each function (e.g., Police = E62 + F62). Sixteen categories cover nearly every functional code reported by these 50 cities; utility enterprises (water, electric, gas, transit) and liquor stores are excluded throughout as self-funded, not general-tax-supported. "Other / NEC" is a catch-all for smaller or inconsistently-reported functions (air transport, corrections in states where cities don't run jails, education support, hospitals) and is large for a few consolidated governments — notably New York and Philadelphia — that fund welfare, hospital, and education-adjacent programs directly in ways most peer cities don't; treat cross-city comparison of that one bucket with caution.

Elasticities (§04): each category's elasticity is fit independently on cities reporting non-zero spending in that category (sample sizes range 15–50; shown per bar). A handful of cities report $0 for functions like corrections or parking because a county or special district handles that function instead of the city — this is a real feature of how U.S. local government fragments responsibility, not missing data, but it does mean the smaller-n categories (corrections, n=15) are less reliable than the near-universal ones (police, fire, n=50).

Partial correlations (§05): computed on log-transformed variables via residualization (regress each variable on the "control" variable, correlate the residuals). This is the standard method but assumes the controlled-for relationship is linear in logs, which held reasonably well on inspection here.

Home value, income, and effective tax rate (§06): pulled from the ACS 2022 5-year estimates — median household income (B19013), median home value (B25077), and median real estate taxes paid (B25103), all at the place level. "Effective tax rate" is median real estate taxes paid ÷ median home value; this is a city-wide median-on-median ratio, not an average of individual households' rates, so it's a solid apples-to-apples comparison across cities but won't match any single homeowner's actual bill exactly.

Single-family housing share (§03): from the same ACS 2022 5-year pull, table B25024 ("Units in Structure"). The headline figure used throughout is "1-unit, detached" only (freestanding single-family homes); attached single-family (townhomes/rowhouses) is pulled separately but not used in §03's headline numbers, since detached-only is the more conservative, more commonly used definition. Same place-level geography as §06, so the same three cities are affected (next note).

Missing consolidated governments: Louisville/Jefferson County, Honolulu, and Nashville-Davidson don't have ACS place-level estimates under the same geographic code used for the finance data, so they're absent from §03 and §06 (n=47 instead of 50 in both). Everything else in the dashboard (density, spending, service categories, §04–§05) is unaffected, since those come from the government finance survey, not the ACS.

What's still open: §03 tested housing type (single-family share) as a sharper alternative to density for explaining spending, and §06 tested home value for explaining the tax rate — but income and home value haven't yet been added as covariates to the §02/§04/§05 spending models directly. Given how much of density's apparent effect turned out to run through housing type in §03, it's plausible some of what's left would run through income too. Adding median household income to those models is the natural next step.
