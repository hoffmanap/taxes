Does Density Pay For Itself?
Population Density, Land Area & the Cost of City Government

A statistical look at whether denser cities deliver municipal services more cheaply per resident, whether that shows up in the property tax bill, and whether the answer differs service by service. The 150 largest U.S. cities, ranked by 2022 population.

Cities analyzed: 150 Service categories: 16 Finance data: U.S. Census Bureau, 2022 Census of Governments, Individual Unit Finance File Geography: 2023 Census Gazetteer (land area)

01Headline Result

A framing note before the findings: throughout this analysis, per-capita spending is treated as a proxy for the level of municipal service a resident actually receives, not simply a "cost." A city spending twice as much per resident is, functionally, providing something closer to twice the police staffing, parks upkeep, library hours, and administrative capacity per resident (setting aside pure efficiency differences, which §04 dissects service-by-service). Read that way, the findings below are less about which cities are "expensive" and more about which cities deliver more government per resident, and which deliver less. One important limit on that proxy: "more/less service" compares the same category label across cities ranging from 175,000 to 8.25 million people, and a label like "Libraries" doesn't guarantee the same kind of institution at every size of city, see §11 for why this matters most for the policy discussion in §06.

The relationship runs the opposite direction from the density-efficiency hypothesis, but only in aggregate. Across the 150 largest cities, higher density is associated with cities that deliver more service per resident, not less, and collect more property tax per resident to fund it. But pooling every service together hides real differentiation: some services show no density penalty at all (§04), the tax relationship turns out to be almost entirely explained by the spending relationship rather than an independent effect of density itself (§05), and a city's housing mix, not density itself, turns out to be the sharper explanation for how much service a city delivers (§03). Separately, §06 shows the fullest picture: low home values and a heavy single-family housing mix combine to push the effective property tax rate up in less-dense cities, even though those same cities are delivering below-average service for the money, which is exactly El Paso's situation. Business density behaves the same way population density does: it looks meaningful on its own but adds nothing once housing type is in the picture, and more jobs nearby does not lower a homeowner's tax rate (§07). Real property tax records across every Texas city in the sample tell a consistent, if more modest, version of the same story: El Paso's commercial tax base is ordinary for a large Texas city, while its multifamily housing share is the lowest of any of its Texas peers, and that gap tracks the tax rate more closely than commercial development does, though not strongly enough on its own to call it confirmed (§08).

03A Sharper Lens Than Density: Housing Type

The question here: "density" (people per square mile) treats a city of small single-family lots and a city of apartment towers the same, as long as they pack in the same number of people. But those are very different things to build and service. So we also pulled the share of each city's housing that's single-family detached homes, the more direct stand-in for "spread-out, low-rise, one-house-per-lot" versus "compact, multifamily, built-up", and tested it the same way.

It works even better than density does. The share of a city's homes that are single-family explains municipal spending per person more precisely than density does (45% of the city-to-city variation in total spending vs. 11% for density; 31% vs. 7% for the density-sensitive service bundle from §04). When both are put in the same model together, density's own effect doesn't just fade, it actually tips slightly negative (though not quite past the conventional significance bar, p≈0.08 to 0.13), while housing type stays strong and clearly significant in both models. In plain terms: it was never really "density" driving the cost pattern. It was housing type. Density looked like it mattered because dense cities also tend to be apartment-heavy cities, but housing type is the more fundamental variable, and once you account for it, density on its own leans toward the opposite direction, not just toward nothing.
The direction is simple: cities with a higher share of single-family homes spend less per person, meaning residents there get less city service per capita, not more. Cities built more around apartments and multifamily housing spend, and deliver, more. That's consistent with everything in §02 through §04. This is also the opposite of the common "sprawl costs more" intuition. In this data, the single-family, lower-density cities are the ones delivering less government per resident, at least at the level of the whole city budget.

On the effective tax rate: housing type does add something to home value's explanation of the tax rate (§06). Home value alone explains about 28% of the city-to-city difference in effective tax rate, and adding housing type on top is statistically significant (p=0.008), running toward a slightly lower rate at a given home value. Housing type's clearest, most consistent role is on the spending side of the analysis (§02 through §04); on the tax-rate side, its effect is smaller and runs counter to the home-value channel rather than alongside it.

04By Service Type: Which Services Scale Up With Density?

What "density elasticity" means, in plain terms: it's the answer to "if density goes up 1%, what happens to the amount of this service delivered per resident, in percent terms?" A value of +1.0 means service delivery rises exactly in step with density (double the density, double the service on that category). A value of +0.3 means it rises much more slowly than density, density has to triple for that category's per-resident delivery to rise about 40%. A value near 0 means density and service level are unrelated, residents get about the same amount of that service no matter how dense the city is. There are no negative values anywhere in this dataset, meaning no service category delivers less per resident as density rises (see the bar chart below for the full ranked list).

In plain terms: we tested all 16 categories of city spending, used as the proxy for service delivered, per §01, separately against density. Not one of them delivers less per resident as density rises. They split into two groups instead, services that deliver more per resident as a city gets denser, and services where density makes no measurable difference either way. There is no category, in this data, where being denser means residents get less of it.

Bottom line: the original idea, pack people in tighter and each person's share of the water main or the road gets cheaper, so a denser city could deliver the same service for less, doesn't show up as savings anywhere in this data. At best (sewers, parks, roads, trash, health) density is a wash: no more service, no less. Everywhere else, and especially in jails, parking, libraries, housing programs, and general administration, being denser means delivering more service per person, not less. That's why total service provision in §02 rises with density: residents of denser cities are getting more government capacity per person, concentrated in the administrative and social-service side of the budget rather than physical infrastructure. An important caveat on what "more" and "less" mean here: this compares the same named category (e.g. "Police") across very differently-sized and differently-scoped cities. A large historic city and a newer, smaller, single-family-dominated city aren't necessarily offering the same depth or type of service within a category just because both report spending under "Housing & Community Development" or "Libraries", see §11 for why this matters for how "less service" should be read.

05Density, Service Provision, and Tax Together

The question here: is the tax-bill pattern in §02 really about density, or is it just riding along with how much service a city provides? We already know three things move together: denser cities deliver more service per person (§02), and cities that deliver more service also collect more property tax per person, no surprise there, since taxes are largely how that service gets funded. The question is whether density is doing anything on its own to push tax bills up, separate from service level, or whether it's only showing up in the tax numbers because it's already showing up in the service numbers.

To test that, we hold service level (spending) constant and ask: once two cities provide the same amount of service per person, does the denser one still collect more tax? The answer is no.

In plain terms: density does not directly drive tax bills. It drives service provision, and service provision drives tax bills. Once you compare cities delivering about the same amount of service per person, density stops predicting property tax per person almost entirely, the connection all but disappears. But service level itself still strongly predicts tax bills even after accounting for density. So the real chain is: denser city → delivers more service per person → collects more property tax per person. Density doesn't have its own separate effect on your tax bill, it only matters because of what it does to service level. If you found two cities delivering the same amount of service per resident, you would not expect the denser one to have a higher tax bill just because it's denser.

(For the statistically inclined: this was tested with partial correlations. Density vs. spending, controlling for tax, stays strong and significant. Density vs. tax, controlling for spending, drops to essentially zero and stops being statistically significant. Spending vs. tax, controlling for density, stays strong. That pattern, one link survives, the other one vanishes, is what "service level explains it, density doesn't" looks like in the numbers.)

06What Actually Drives the Tax Burden: A Combined Story

The question behind this section: a city's property tax bill and its property tax rate are two different things. The bill is dollars collected per person. The rate is the bill divided by what the home is actually worth. By definition, if a city needs to raise a given amount of tax revenue per resident and its housing stock isn't worth very much, the rate has to be higher to get there, that part is just arithmetic, not a statistical finding. The real question is what makes a city's housing stock worth less in the first place, and whether density and housing type play a genuine role in that or are beside the point. They do play a role, a real, measurable, secondary one.

Three things determine the rate, in order of how much they matter: a city's income level dominates home values (r = 0.90, by far the strongest relationship in this entire analysis), single-family housing share matters independently on top of income (p < 0.0001, this is the density/housing-type channel), and home value in turn is what mechanically sets the effective rate (r = -0.42). Put together: income sets the baseline, housing type pulls it down further, and the combination determines how hard the rate has to work. On their own, housing type and density don't show a strong direct relationship with the tax rate (r ≈ 0.05, not significant either way). Most of their effect runs through home value, not around it. One genuine wrinkle: once you hold home value fixed, single-family share shows a small but statistically real direct relationship with the rate (p = 0.008), and it runs the opposite direction, toward a slightly lower rate. The likely reason isn't mysterious: at a given home value, a more single-family city needs less total revenue (§03, it delivers less service), so it can charge a bit less to raise that smaller amount. That's a real, separate channel, but a small one next to the value channel, and it works against El Paso's situation rather than adding to it, which makes the value-driven story below the conservative, not the inflated, read.
El Paso is close to the worst-case combination this data can produce. Its income is low (119th of 147, in the bottom fifth), which alone would predict a fairly low home value. Its housing stock is also heavily single-family (66.9%, 25th-highest share of 147 cities, top sixth of the sample), and single-family share keeps pulling value down even after income is accounted for. The result: El Paso's actual median home value ($159,300) is 22% below what its income alone would predict. Less taxable value per resident, for two compounding reasons instead of one, means El Paso needs an unusually high rate just to raise an ordinary amount of money, 5th-highest effective tax rate of 147 cities, 2.17%, even though its actual dollar tax bill per resident lands almost exactly in the middle of the pack (80th of 147).

And per §02–§04's reframing, that same housing pattern means El Paso is also on the low end of service delivery: direct spending per resident ranks 22nd-lowest of 147, about 65% of the citywide median. So the full picture isn't just "El Paso's rate is high." It's El Paso residents pay a higher rate to fund below-average service, and the same underlying housing pattern, low-value, low-density, heavily single-family, is doing double duty: it's why the city delivers less (§02–§04), and it's part of why raising even that reduced amount of money requires a higher rate (this section). Denser cities with a larger multifamily share don't just deliver more service, they raise their bigger budgets at a lower rate, because their housing stock carries more taxable value per acre to spread it across.

The definitive answer, put in one number: El Paso's actual median home value ($159,300) is 22% below what its income alone would predict ($203,954). Holding El Paso's actual tax bill and income effect fixed, and asking what its rate would be if that single-family-driven gap in home value simply didn't exist, the math is direct: $3,461 in taxes paid ÷ $203,954 in home value = 1.70%, not the actual 2.17%. Closing the single-family housing gap alone would cut El Paso's effective tax rate by about 22%, closing roughly 43% of the entire gap between El Paso and the average large city's rate (1.07%). The rest of that gap is El Paso's income level itself, which is a far bigger lever on home value (r≈0.86) than housing type is (r≈0.27), but housing type is the one lever a city actually controls through zoning and permitting; income is not.
The policy-relevant version of this finding: if El Paso wants a lower effective tax rate without cutting services further, the data point at a specific, actionable lever, shift new construction and infill away from single-family detached housing and toward multifamily and higher-density formats. Two independent results in this analysis both point the same direction: (1) each additional percentage point of a city's housing stock that's single-family is associated with roughly 0.8% lower home value, holding income constant (§09, row 9), so less reliance on single-family means more taxable value per acre, which is what lowers the rate needed to raise any given amount of revenue; and (2) each additional percentage point of single-family share is associated with roughly 3% lower per-capita spending in the same category dollars (§03). These aren't two separate policy trade-offs to weigh against each other, in this data, they trace back to the same underlying shift in taxable value per acre. What's solid: more taxable value and more revenue collected is a direct, mechanical consequence of this shift, not a hopeful extrapolation. What's less certain: whether that extra revenue would translate into the same kind of expanded civic services that older, larger cities in this sample provide, or into a proportionally larger version of what El Paso already offers, the data can't fully distinguish those, for the reasons in §11's first caveat. Either way, "more revenue, funding more of something" is the defensible claim; "more revenue funding the same breadth of services a much larger legacy city offers" is a plausible but unproven extension of it.

Does the Type of Multifamily Housing Matter?

The ACS breaks housing structures down further than just "single-family vs. not": duplexes, 3-4 unit buildings, 5-9, 10-19, 20-49, and 50+ unit structures are each tracked separately. That lets us test whether "missing middle" housing (duplex through 9-unit buildings, roughly single-family-lot scale, and generally the easier kind of infill for a city to permit in existing neighborhoods) does the same work as larger apartment buildings (10+ units). El Paso is currently 13.7% missing-middle and 12.6% large multifamily, both below the sample average (16.5% and 21.5%).

The honest bottom line: missing-middle housing is not a lesser lever than large apartment buildings for service delivery. That's a confirmed result, not an absence of evidence to the contrary. For the tax-rate side, both structure sizes clearly matter on their own, and the difference between them isn't statistically confirmed. Practically: there's solid evidence that missing-middle infill (duplexes, small apartment buildings) is a productive lever for El Paso in its own right, not just an unproven alternative to large apartment construction, while likely remaining the easier political and physical lift in existing single-family neighborhoods.

07Does Business Density Matter the Way Population Density Does?

The question here: everything so far measures residential density. Does a city's concentration of jobs, its business density, behave the same way? We pulled total employment by workplace location from the Census Bureau's LEHD LODES data (the same federal source behind El Paso's own job growth work) for 146 of the 150 cities, and computed jobs per square mile as the direct commercial analog to population density.

Jobs density is a real, measurable thing, and it moves closely with population density (r = 0.86). On its own, jobs density actually predicts spending per capita a bit better than population density does (31% of the variation explained versus 17%). But once housing type is added to the model, jobs density stops adding anything: its own effect drops to essentially zero and stops being significant, the same pattern population density showed in §03. Jobs density is not an independent lever on spending. It is riding on the same underlying housing-type story as everything else in this analysis.
Jobs density does not lower the homeowner's tax rate. If anything, it points the other way. The simple relationship between jobs per resident and the effective tax rate is not significant (r = -0.09, p = 0.30). Tested more carefully, holding home value constant, jobs density is significantly associated with a higher rate, not a lower one (p = 0.002). The likely reason: cities with more jobs per acre tend to have higher home values for reasons unrelated to jobs themselves (land scarcity, regional economics), and once that's accounted for, having more jobs nearby does not translate into tax relief for residents. El Paso's own jobs-per-resident ratio is 0.42, below the sample median of 0.53, placing it 108th of 146 cities, meaning most large cities run a more job-dense economy relative to their population than El Paso does. That is a real fact about El Paso's economy. It is a separate fact from whether more jobs would lower the tax rate, and this data says it would not, at least not on its own.

One limit worth stating plainly: jobs density measures economic activity, not commercial property value. A city can have many jobs in leased office space without much of that showing up as local commercial tax base. The next section uses real property assessment data instead of this jobs proxy, and tells a more precise version of the same story.

08Commercial Tax Base: What the Real Numbers Show

The question here: a common idea in El Paso is that the city should shift its tax base toward businesses and away from homeowners. To test this properly requires the actual split between residential and commercial assessed property value, which the federal datasets used everywhere else in this analysis do not report. Property classification is a state and county function. We pulled it directly from the Texas Comptroller's Appraisal District Ratio Study, the state's own official value-by-category report, for every Texas city in the 150-city sample: 18 cities across 12 counties. Several cities share a county (Fort Worth and Arlington both sit in Tarrant; Dallas, Irving, Garland, and Grand Prairie all sit in Dallas County; Plano, Frisco, and McKinney all sit in Collin County), so the property-mix figures are identical within those groups even though each city's own effective tax rate differs.

The honest read: multifamily share is still the more consistent story here, clearly more so than commercial share, but this Texas comparison alone isn't large enough to call the relationship confirmed. It's a real, plausible pattern worth weighing alongside the much larger and more statistically solid finding in §03 and §06, not a standalone proof on its own.

One data point worth flagging rather than smoothing over: Laredo (Webb County) has an unusual property profile, only 38% of its taxable value is residential at all, with a large "other" category (35%) that doesn't fit neatly into the residential/commercial split used elsewhere in this table, likely reflecting Laredo's role as a major international trade and logistics hub. Its numbers are included above for completeness, but its effective tax rate (2.03%, the second-highest in this Texas comparison after El Paso) may be shaped by factors this table doesn't capture well.

This section covers 18 cities rather than the full 150 because property classification data is not standardized nationally, each state and county reports differently, and no single federal source covers it the way the Census of Governments finance data or the ACS covers everything else in this analysis. The scope here is intentionally narrower and more direct: real numbers for El Paso and every other Texas city in the sample, rather than a proxy applied everywhere.

09What Survives the Controls

This is the robustness summary for the whole analysis, every regression model referenced in §02 through §06, side by side, so the density/service/tax/housing-type/income relationships can be checked against each other in one place rather than taken on faith from the prose above. 11Method & Caveats

Do mid-size and large cities actually provide comparable services? Probably not fully, and this is the most important limitation in the whole analysis. Every finding here compares the same 16 named Census functional categories (Police, Libraries, Housing & Community Development, etc.) across 150 cities ranging from 175,000 to 8.25 million people, a 47x range. The category label being identical doesn't mean what's delivered under it is comparable: a "Libraries" line for a smaller, newer, single-family-dominated Sunbelt city might mean a couple of branch locations with limited hours, while the same label for a large historic city can mean an extensive multi-branch system built up over a century. The same goes for Housing & Community Development, General Government, and several of the other categories that showed the strongest density/housing-type elasticities in §04, these are exactly the categories where large legacy cities have built out the most extensive civic infrastructure (homelessness services, public housing agencies, large permitting bureaucracies), often infrastructure that smaller or newer cities never built, in some cases by deliberate low-tax, minimal-government design rather than simple underfunding.

This is a real, confirmed pattern in the data, not a hypothetical: single-family share is significantly correlated with population size itself (r=−0.34, p<0.0001), smaller cities in this sample are systematically more single-family-dominated. That means "housing type" and "city age/scale/historical role" move together, and this analysis can't fully separate them. So "single-family-dominant cities deliver less service" is solid as a description of dollars spent, that part is just arithmetic on real government finance data. It's less certain as a causal claim that changing El Paso's housing mix would cause it to build the same kind of civic institutions that older, larger cities have. It's equally possible that a city like El Paso could increase its multifamily share substantially and still never develop, say, an extensive public library system or homelessness-services apparatus, if what's really driving those institutions in the data is a city's age and historical role rather than its current housing stock. The safer, better-supported claim, and the one this analysis leans on for the policy discussion in §06, is the narrower one: more taxable value and more tax revenue collected, which is a real, mechanical fact regardless of what specific services that revenue eventually funds. The broader "and therefore more/better civic services" reading should be treated as a plausible hypothesis this data is consistent with, not a proven mechanism.

Service categories: built from Census of Governments item codes, combining current-operations and capital-construction spending for each function (e.g., Police = E62 + F62). Sixteen categories cover nearly every functional code reported by these 150 cities; utility enterprises (water, electric, gas, transit) and liquor stores are excluded throughout as self-funded, not general-tax-supported. "Other / NEC" is a catch-all for smaller or inconsistently-reported functions (air transport, corrections in states where cities don't run jails, education support, hospitals) and is large for a few consolidated governments, notably New York and Philadelphia, that fund welfare, hospital, and education-adjacent programs directly in ways most peer cities don't; treat cross-city comparison of that one bucket with caution.

Elasticities (§04): each category's elasticity is fit independently on cities reporting non-zero spending in that category (sample sizes range 35 to 150; shown per bar). A handful of cities report $0 for functions like corrections or parking because a county or special district handles that function instead of the city. This is a real feature of how U.S. local government fragments responsibility, not missing data, but it does mean the smaller-n categories (corrections, n=35) are less reliable than the near-universal ones (police, n=149; parks, n=150). Corrections in particular is worth reading with caution: it is the category with the fewest reporting cities, and its relationship with density is not statistically significant here, a reminder that categories with thin reporting deserve less confidence than the near-universal ones.

Partial correlations (§05): computed on log-transformed variables via residualization (regress each variable on the "control" variable, correlate the residuals). This is the standard method but assumes the controlled-for relationship is linear in logs, which held reasonably well on inspection here.

Home value, income, and effective tax rate (§06): pulled from the ACS 2022 5-year estimates, median household income (B19013), median home value (B25077), and median real estate taxes paid (B25103), all at the place level. "Effective tax rate" is median real estate taxes paid divided by median home value; this is a city-wide median-on-median ratio, not an average of individual households' rates, so it's a solid apples-to-apples comparison across cities but won't match any single homeowner's actual bill exactly.

Single-family housing share (§03): from the same ACS 2022 5-year pull, table B25024 ("Units in Structure"). The headline figure used throughout is "1-unit, detached" only (freestanding single-family homes); attached single-family (townhomes/rowhouses) is pulled separately but not used in §03's headline numbers, since detached-only is the more conservative, more commonly used definition. Same place-level geography as §06, so the same three cities are affected (next note).

Jobs density (§07): from the Census Bureau's LEHD LODES workplace employment data, 2021, the most recent year available consistently across the states in this sample. Alaska is excluded because its LODES data has not been updated since 2016, too stale to mix with the rest.

Commercial and multifamily tax base (§08): from the Texas Comptroller's Appraisal District Ratio Study, the state's official property value by category report, for El Paso, Austin, Dallas, Fort Worth, Houston, and San Antonio. El Paso, Bexar, Dallas, and Travis counties report on the 2024 tax year; Harris and Tarrant counties report on 2023, the most recent year available for each under the state's biennial reporting cycle. This section covers six cities rather than 150 because property classification is not standardized nationally.

Missing consolidated governments: Louisville/Jefferson County, Honolulu, and Nashville-Davidson don't have ACS place-level estimates under the same geographic code used for the finance data, so they're absent from §03 and §06 (n=147 instead of 150). Anchorage is present in most sections but absent from §07 for the LODES data-vintage reason above. Everything else in the dashboard (density, spending, service categories, §04 and §05) is unaffected, since those come from the government finance survey, not the ACS.

Income as a covariate: median household income adds essentially nothing on top of housing type for the spending models (R² is unchanged to three decimal places for total spending, p=0.98 for income's own effect; barely moves for the density-sensitive bundle, p=0.66). Housing type is the dominant, overwhelmingly significant predictor throughout (p<10⁻¹⁶) with or without income in the model, and density's own effect stays weak, leaning slightly negative rather than toward zero (p≈0.07), though not quite past the conventional significance threshold. Income is not a missing piece that would change the §03 conclusion.

Why 150 cities: the sample covers the 150 largest U.S. cities by population. This size was chosen to give the missing-middle versus large-multifamily test in §06 enough statistical power to produce a real answer rather than an inconclusive one (see §06, §09 rows 10 and 11). One tradeoff worth naming: the raw density-vs-spending relationship in §02 is real but modest (R² around 0.10 to 0.17 depending on specification), while the housing-type story in §03 is considerably stronger and more consistent. Both are genuine findings; they simply carry different amounts of explanatory weight.
